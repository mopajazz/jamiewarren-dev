# Causal World Framework

## Definition of tiny

A Tiny Causal World is the smallest bounded, replayable system in which a learner can act on one primary causal relationship, observe its consequences, and revise their mental model.

“Tiny” describes the explanatory unit, not the screen count, duration, file size, fidelity, or seriousness of the subject.

A world is sufficiently small when:

- it investigates one primary causal question;
- every included variable is necessary to explain that relationship;
- action produces legible feedback quickly enough to support prediction and comparison;
- the learner can predict, act, observe, revise, and replay;
- boundaries, assumptions, and simplifications are explicit; and
- removing anything else would make the model false or instructionally weaker.

Default to one to three learner-controlled variables at a time. Additional state may remain latent or appear progressively. Exceed this default only when fewer controls would materially falsify or conceal the relationship. Decompose complex subjects into layered or connected Tiny Causal Worlds rather than one cognitively opaque simulation.

## Four connected models

### Conceptual model

Define the domain truth the learner should internalize:

- entities and relevant state;
- causal variables and directional relationships;
- rules, constraints, thresholds, delays, and feedback loops;
- initial conditions and system boundaries;
- uncertainty or randomness when it is part of the truth;
- assumptions and deliberate simplifications.

Express the primary causal claim in a testable form such as: “When X changes under conditions C, Y changes through mechanism M.”

### Simulation model

Decide what the learner can do and what the system reveals:

- learner-controlled variables;
- state that changes without direct control;
- immediate, delayed, cumulative, or probabilistic consequences;
- visible signals that make latent processes inferable;
- reset, replay, comparison, and counterfactual behavior;
- failure, equilibrium, saturation, or completion conditions.

The system should respond coherently even when the learner does something unproductive. Feedback should arise from consequences where possible; explanatory text should clarify the model rather than substitute for it.

### Instructional model

Connect the world to learning:

- audience and prerequisite knowledge;
- aligned outcome or assessed performance;
- likely misconception, fragile intuition, or decision error;
- prediction prompt before manipulation;
- scaffolding that fades as the model becomes legible;
- reflection or explanation that demonstrates the learner's revised model;
- transfer beyond the world to a new case or authentic task.

Do not add scoring, narrative, competition, badges, or game mechanics unless they strengthen attention to the causal relationship.

### Experience model

Define how the world communicates its model:

- information hierarchy and spatial arrangement;
- interaction vocabulary and perceived affordances;
- visual encoding for state, change, magnitude, time, and consequence;
- motion, sound, texture, and language only when they clarify causality;
- emotional register appropriate to the learner, subject, and stakes;
- one faithfully applied bundled design system.

## Common causal structures

Look for flow, accumulation, equilibrium, thresholds, delays, reinforcing or balancing feedback, tradeoffs, competition, networks, probability, resource constraints, and path dependence. Select an interaction that exposes the actual structure rather than decorating the subject.

## Truthfulness tests

Reject or revise the model when:

- the learner can succeed without noticing the causal relationship;
- feedback rewards task completion but does not reveal why the system changed;
- the controls are unrelated to authentic decisions or mechanisms;
- simplification reverses, hides, or falsely implies the domain relationship;
- more text is required because the system itself remains illegible;
- visual spectacle competes with observation;
- the activity is merely recall, sorting, or click-to-reveal with a simulation skin.

The world is ready for the World Gate when a learner could form a prediction, run a meaningful test, compare the result, and explain the governing relationship.
